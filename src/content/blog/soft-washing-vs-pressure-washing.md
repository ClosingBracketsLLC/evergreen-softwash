Walk any waterfront neighborhood in the Sound and you can spot the houses that were pressure washed by an enthusiastic amateur: fuzzy streaks across the cedar, chalky stripes on the render, a roof shedding granules into the gutters. The tool wasn't wrong — it was just pointed at the wrong surface.

Here's the distinction that professionals use, and that every homeowner should know before hiring anyone with a wand.

## The one-sentence rule

**If the surface is hard mineral underfoot — concrete, pavers, natural stone — pressure wash it. If it's anything else — siding, roofs, fences, screens, painted or sealed wood — soft wash it.**

## What soft washing actually is

[Soft washing](/services/soft-washing/) applies a biodegradable cleaning solution at little more than garden-hose pressure — typically under 100 PSI, versus the 2,500–4,000 PSI of a pressure washer. The chemistry does the work: it dissolves the algae, mold, mildew, moss, and lichen that make surfaces green and black, then everything rinses away gently.

The crucial difference is *what happens at the root*. Pressure shears growth off at the surface, leaving the organism's holdfast alive in the substrate. Chemistry kills it entirely. That's why a soft-washed roof stays clean four to six years while a pressure-washed one greens up again in twelve to eighteen months.

## What pressure washing is for

Concrete, brick, and stone can take real pressure — and often need it. Embedded grime, tire marks, and years of oxidized dirt don't respond to gentle chemistry alone. The professional difference is equipment and technique: [surface cleaners](/services/pressure-washing/) that spread pressure evenly (no zebra stripes), PSI matched to the material, and pre-treatment for oil, rust, and tannin stains.

## Where homeowners get burned

- **Roofs.** Walking a roof with a pressure wand strips granules from composite shingles and cracks tiles. Every major shingle manufacturer specifies low-pressure chemical cleaning; high pressure can void the warranty outright.
- **Cedar and softwoods.** Pressure "fuzzes" the grain permanently. Once the fibers lift, only sanding fixes it.
- **Painted siding.** Pressure finds every weak spot in the paint film and peels it — often injecting water behind the cladding on the way.
- **Window seals.** Direct high pressure blows out glazing seals, and fogged double-panes follow a season later.

## What a professional visit looks like

Most complete exterior projects use both methods in sequence: soft wash for the house envelope and roof, pressure washing for the drives, walks, and patios. That's why we quote them together — and why the crew that arrives carries both systems on one rig.

The honest answer to "which do I need?" is usually "both, in the right places." What matters is that whoever you hire knows the difference — and can explain, before they start, exactly which method every surface on your property is going to get.
