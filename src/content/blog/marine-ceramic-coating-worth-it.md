Ceramic coating arrived in the marine world with the same breathless marketing it had in the car world, which makes it hard to get a straight answer to the only question that matters: *is it worth it on your boat?*

Having coated a lot of hulls — and talked plenty of owners out of it — here's how we actually think about it.

## What a marine ceramic coating does

A professional ceramic system bonds a hard, glass-like layer to your gelcoat or paint. On the water, that buys you three real things:

- **UV resistance.** The coating takes the sun's abuse instead of your gelcoat, dramatically slowing the chalking that dulls hulls.
- **Release.** Salt crust, waterline scum, exhaust staining, and guano release with a rinse instead of a scrub. Owners consistently report wash effort cut in half.
- **Gloss retention.** A coated hull holds its depth of shine for seasons, not months.

What it does *not* do: prevent scratches, replace bottom paint, or make a neglected hull look restored. Coating locks in whatever finish exists the day it's applied — which is why [correction comes first](/services/watercraft-detailing/), always.

## When it clearly pays

**Boats that live in the water.** Moored vessels fight waterline staining and biological growth all season. This is where ceramic earns its keep most obviously — the waterline wipes clean instead of demanding acid washes that eat gelcoat.

**Dark hulls.** Navy, black, and deep green hulls show oxidation first and suffer UV worst. Coating a freshly corrected dark hull is the difference between "stunning for years" and "chalky by August."

**Owners who pay for wash service.** If a crew washes your boat weekly, the coating cuts labor per visit. Over a multi-year coating life, the arithmetic often goes positive on labor savings alone.

## When wax is the smarter buy

**Trailered boats under 25 feet.** Out of the water, rinsed after use, garage-kept? A quality polymer sealant twice a season keeps that boat beautiful for a fraction of the cost.

**Hulls due for paint.** Coating gelcoat that's within a couple seasons of an Awlgrip job is money thrown overboard. Spend it on the paint.

**"Flip" boats.** Selling in a year? A correction and wax presents just as well at survey.

## The honest numbers

For a professional system on Puget Sound, expect roughly **$95–$150 per foot** including the correction work, depending on condition and vessel type, with a serviceable life of two to four seasons in-water. Against an annual compound-and-wax at $55–$70 per foot, coating typically breaks even around season two — *if* the boat stays in the water and *if* the prep was done right.
